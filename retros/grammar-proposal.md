# Grammar proposal — can the machine propose the *grammar*?

The law-proposal retro (`law-proposal.md`) closed the *enumerate* half
of "the AI invents laws": a hand-written grammar of 23 algebraic
schemas, of which 9 distinct are genuinely-new and useful.  Its own
caveat was the thing left standing:

> the schema grammar is human-authored; the machine enumerates,
> verifies and prices what the grammar generates; the creativity is
> still the grammar.

This retro attacks that caveat.  `tools/grammar_proposal.py` treats a
**schema as data** — a shape over the op algebra — and searches the
*shape space*: it mutates and recombines shapes, instantiates each into
a concrete law, and scores it with the same oracles `law_proposal.py`
uses (`law_verifier.verify_law`, the numeric truth oracle, and the
cost-delta usefulness test).

The headline is a **bounded positive and a clear negative**:

* the search *does* find useful schemas the hand-written grammar does
  not generate — but only **two**, and both are the grammar's own
  laws *transferred to a new op* (`div`), not a new structural shape;
* the search **does not beat enumeration**: the human grammar yields
  0.478 useful laws per verification, the search 0.010 — roughly
  **50x worse**;
* going deeper buys nothing: depth 1 already finds both novel schemas;
  depths 2 and 3 add zero.

Reproduce: `.venv/bin/python tools/grammar_proposal.py`
(~75 s, CPU-only, no network; `--json PATH` for machine-readable
output).  Output is deterministic across runs and `PYTHONHASHSEED`
values (the search order is a fixed neighbour enumeration plus
alpha-normal schema keys, never a hash-ordered set).

## 1. A schema as data

A `Shape` is a small frozen tree: an op node (`op`, `children`) or a
leaf.  A leaf is either a **slot** (an integer standing for a repeated
subterm — two leaves with the same slot index denote the *same*
concrete subterm when instantiated) or a **literal** `Const`.  A
*schema* is a pair of shapes `(lhs, rhs)`; the grammar's own law
`add(mul x,y),(mul x,z)) -> mul(x, add(y,z))` is the shape
`add(mul(s0,s1), mul(s0,s2)) -> mul(s0, add(s1,s2))` — the sharing
pattern (`s0` in both products) is explicit.

Instantiation maps each slot to a shape-correct `Var` (4x4) and rebuilds
the term; `verify_law`, the numeric oracle and the cost-delta test are
then applied exactly as `law_proposal.py` applies them.  Nothing under
`packages/` changes.

## 2. The search

The **seeds** are the hand-written grammar itself: `law_proposal`'s 23
candidates abstracted to shapes (distinct leaves → distinct slots,
`Const` stays literal).  A bounded breadth-first search then expands
each shape through one-step **mutation operators**:

| operator | what it does |
|---|---|
| swap | exchange a binary node's operands |
| op-swap | replace a node's op by another of the same arity |
| wrap / unwrap | add / remove a unary op at a leaf / node |
| share / split | merge two slots, or split one repeated slot |
| literal | substitute `0`/`1`/`2` for a leaf, or promote one to a slot |
| cross | recombine one schema's LHS with another's RHS |

Two well-formedness filters keep the space a *law* space: the RHS may
introduce no slot absent from the LHS (`meta._synthesizable`'s
condition), and the search never emits a shape that violates it.

A schema is scored by the four `law_proposal` verdicts — **derivable**
(proved from `ALL_RULES`), **num-true** (both sides agree on random
fp64 tensors), **genuinely new** (not a duplicate/inverse of a library
rule), **useful** (true *and* lowering an extracted cost under
`flops_cost`).  The search's objective is *useful & new*.

## 3. What "the grammar does not generate it" means

This is the load-bearing definition, and getting it wrong produces
false positives.  Three near-misses were found and closed:

1. **Commutativity.**  `add(mul(x,z),mul(x,y)) -> mul(x,add(y,z))` is
   `mul_factor` with the products swapped — the grammar *does* generate
   it (via the library's `comm_mul`/`comm_add`).  Comparing shapes up
   to coherence is not enough either: `canonicalize`'s child ordering
   is name-dependent, so it is not alpha-invariant.  The fix is
   semantic, not structural: **a law is generated by the grammar iff
   the grammar's laws — abstracted to patterns — together with the
   library *derive* it** (a bounded saturation in a fresh e-graph).
2. **Instances.**  `mul(neg x, 0) -> 0` is the grammar's `mul_zero`
   applied to the subterm `neg x`; as a *pattern* the grammar's law
   rewrites it in one step.  A shape-level test that misses instances
   is too weak; the saturation test catches them.
3. **Composites.**  `square(mul(neg x, y)) -> mul(square x, square y)`
   is `square_mul` ∘ `square_neg` — two grammar laws.  The saturation
   test catches these too.

All three "novel" populations vanished once the closure test was the
saturation one.  The two that survived reach a **fixed point** in the
closure (so the grammar provably cannot derive them within budget).

### A real pitfall the search surfaced

The e-graph keys leaves by `repr`, so `Const(0)` and `Const(0.0)` are
**different leaves**.  A search that substitutes float literals
(`mul(x, 0.0)`) then *misses* the grammar's own `mul(x, 0)` rule and
reports a spurious "new law".  The search uses the grammar's integer
spelling.  This is a genuine sharp edge in the machinery, not a search
artifact.

### Bounded saturation is not optional

The grammar's annihilator laws (`mul(x,0) -> 0`) merge a term with a
*subterm of itself*, so an unbudgeted saturation nests
`mul(x, mul(x, mul(x, …)))` without bound — `verify_law` with its
shipped 200 000-node default saturates for minutes on a five-node term.
Both the closure check and the search's own derivability probe run
under the pipeline's *bounded* policy (per-rule `rule_budgets` + a node
cap).  Bounding cannot change usefulness: a derivable law can never
lower a cost (its members are already reachable), so the `derivable`
flag is cosmetic for the verdict the search optimises.

## 4. Yield — the search vs enumeration

| | verifications | useful & new | useful per verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| human grammar | 23 | 11 | **0.478** |
| search, full (depth 3) | 5711 | 58 | 0.010 |
| search, only (excl. the 23 seeds) | 5688 | 47 | **0.008** |

The search's 58 useful-and-new laws are mostly the grammar's own
schemas rediscovered as *instances* (56 of them are grammar-generated;
only 2 are not).  Read the grammar's 23 laws as patterns and they
generate far more than the 23 listed instances — but that is
rediscovery, not proposal.

**Enumeration wins by ~50x per verification.**  A small hand-written
grammar of textbook identities is simply high-precision; a
neighbourhood search over its mutations is mostly re-proposing
reorderings and instances of what it started from.  This is the honest
answer to "does search beat enumeration": **no**.

## 5. Does the search find a schema the grammar lacks?

Yes — two, both at depth 1 (depths 2 and 3 add nothing):

| law | cost | note |
|---|---|---|
| `div(0, x) -> 0` | 64 → 0 | `div`'s annihilator |
| `div(x, 1) -> x` | 64 → 0 | `div`'s identity |

Both are numerically true, structurally new (no library rule is a `div`
identity/annihilator), and **not derivable** from the grammar + library
(the closure check reaches a fixed point without a merge).  Neither is
a duplicate or inverse of a library rule.

But be honest about *what* they are: they are the grammar's `mul_zero`
/`mul_zero_left` and `pow_one` laws with the op **op-swapped** to
`div`.  The search did not invent a new structural shape; it
transferred an existing shape to an op the human did not write it for.
That is a real (if small) extension of the grammar — the grammar has
`mul`'s annihilator and `pow`'s identity but not `div`'s — and it is
the kind of thing a bounded mutation *can* do.  It is not "the machine
proposing the grammar itself" in any strong sense.

Caveat: both are *conditional* in the same way the grammar's own
`div_self` is — `div(0, x) = 0` needs `x != 0`; `div(x, 1) = x` is
unconditional.  They would need a `check` hook before entering the
library.

## Verdict

* **The grammar caveat stands, sharpened.**  A bounded schema search
  over the human grammar's neighbourhood finds **two** useful laws the
  grammar lacks — and both are op-transfers of grammar laws, not new
  structural shapes.  The creativity is still overwhelmingly in the
  grammar.
* **Search does not beat enumeration.**  0.008 useful-per-verification
  for the search against 0.478 for the 23 hand-written schemas: the
  grammar is ~50x more efficient, because a neighbourhood search mostly
  re-derives reorderings, instances and composites of its seeds.
* **Depth buys nothing here.**  Depth 1 already finds both novel
  schemas; depths 2 and 3 (a further ~4000 verifications) add zero.
  The useful novel region is one mutation from the seeds.
* **The novelty definition is the whole game.**  Three natural but
  wrong definitions (shape equality, coherence-modulo equality,
  concrete-instance equality) each produced false "new laws"
  (commutative variants, grammar instances, composites).  Only
  *derivability from the grammar's laws + the library* is sound.
* **The search found two real machinery edges**: the e-graph's
  `repr`-keyed leaves make `Const(0) != Const(0.0)` (a float spelling
  hides a law from its own rule), and the annihilator laws make an
  unbudgeted saturation nest a term inside itself without bound.

## Caveats (stated, not tuned)

* **The seed set is the human grammar.**  Every "new" schema is a
  mutation of a human schema.  A search from *primitive* seeds only —
  no human grammar — is the stronger test and is not attempted here;
  the neighbourhood of a blank seed set is far larger and the expected
  yield (by the retro's own near-miss and composite results) is worse.
* **The mutation vocabulary is human-chosen.**  Six operators over a
  fixed op set (`add`/`mul`/`sub`/`div`/`pow`/`matmul`, eight unary
  ops).  The "creativity" is now split between the grammar and the
  operators — it has not been eliminated.
* **The numeric oracle is domain-restricted.**  It samples symmetric
  random normals, so domain-restricted laws (`square(sqrt x) = x`,
  `log(exp x) = x`) are rejected as false.  This is inherited from
  `law_proposal.py` and left unchanged; it makes the search's "true"
  column conservative, never optimistic.
* **Novelty is a bounded proof.**  "Not generated by the grammar" is
  "the bounded closure saturation did not merge" — reported with its
  `closure_stop`.  Both novel laws reached a fixed point, so the claim
  is strong for them; a candidate that stopped at `max_nodes` would be
  reported, not silently called novel.
* **Usefulness is `flops_cost` on the law's own LHS instance.**  A
  different cost model, or a whole-model probe, could re-rank.
* **The search's derivability probe is bounded** (see §3), which is why
  the whole tool is ~75 s rather than minutes per candidate.  It cannot
  change the *useful* verdict (a derivable law never lowers a cost).
